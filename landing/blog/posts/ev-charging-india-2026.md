# India's EV Charging Network in 2026: Where We Are and What's Next

India crossed 25,000 public EV charging stations in 2025. The Ministry of Heavy Industries declared it a milestone. Auto publications ran celebratory headlines. And the headlines were not wrong — five years ago, 2,000 stations felt ambitious.

But if you are an EV driver who just tried to find a working fast charger on the Hyderabad–Bengaluru highway at 8 PM on a Friday, the milestone feels somewhat abstract.

Numbers are a starting point, not a destination. Here is a more honest picture of where India's charging infrastructure stands in 2026 — and what is genuinely improving.

---

## The Network by the Numbers

India's 25,000+ charging points are deeply uneven in their distribution:

| State | Public Charging Points | % of National Total |
|-------|----------------------|---------------------|
| Maharashtra | ~5,400 | 21% |
| Delhi & NCR | ~4,100 | 16% |
| Karnataka | ~3,200 | 13% |
| Tamil Nadu | ~2,600 | 10% |
| Telangana | ~1,800 | 7% |
| All others | ~7,900 | 33% |

So five states account for 67% of all public chargers. And within those states, the chargers are further concentrated in a handful of cities. Mumbai, Pune, Bengaluru, Chennai, Delhi, and Hyderabad together account for roughly half of all public charging infrastructure in the country.

This is not a crisis. It is a pattern that mirrors how EV adoption itself has spread — urban-first, then corridors, then deeper into Tier 2 cities.

---

## The Speed Problem

Raw charger counts mask a critical variable: charging speed.

Of India's 25,000+ charging points:
- **~18,000** are AC slow chargers (3.3–7.4 kW) — useful for overnight charging at home or office, but adding only 25–50 km of range per hour
- **~5,500** are AC fast chargers (11–22 kW)
- **~1,800** are DC fast chargers (30–150 kW) — the ones that can add 200 km of range in 30–40 minutes

For long-distance travel, only the DC fast charger network matters. And 1,800 stations — spread across 3.29 million square kilometres of Indian roads — is thin coverage.

The good news: DC fast charger installations grew 340% between 2023 and 2025. The NITI Aayog's target is 10,000 DC fast chargers by 2027. Based on the current installation rate, that number is achievable.

## What Is Actually Improving

### Highway Corridors
The National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) has prioritised charging infrastructure on its key corridors. The Mumbai–Pune Expressway now has fast chargers every 40–50 km. The Delhi–Agra stretch is similarly well-covered. The Bengaluru–Mysuru highway saw four new 120 kW stations come online in early 2026.

Importantly, these are not individual chargers — they are **charging plazas** with multiple bays, shade structures, and often a café or convenience store. The highway-charging experience in India is materially better in 2026 than it was even two years ago.

### Network Reliability
Early public EV chargers in India had notoriously poor uptime — some estimates put failure rates at 30–40%. This was partly a hardware issue (early-generation equipment in an unfamiliar climate) and partly a maintenance issue (no one assigned to fix a broken charger quickly).

The newer installations from Tata Power, Ather Grid, ChargePoint, and Statiq come with remote monitoring, predictive maintenance alerts, and service-level agreements that mandate a response within 4–8 hours. Uptime on major network chargers has improved to 85–92% in surveyed corridors — still not perfect, but meaningfully better.

### Interoperability
In 2023, finding a charger that worked with your specific car's connector was a minor lottery. The Bureau of Indian Standards has since mandated **CCS2 and CHAdeMO compatibility** on all new public fast chargers, with Bharat AC-001 as the standard for slow chargers. Most new EVs sold in India now support at least CCS2.

## What Is Still Hard

### Tier 2 and 3 Cities
Step outside the metros and the picture changes quickly. Warangal, Vizag, Coimbatore, Indore — cities with hundreds of thousands of EV registrations but sparse public charging. Drivers here rely almost entirely on home charging overnight, which works for daily commutes but makes intercity travel difficult.

### Rural and Semi-Urban Highways
The NH-44 corridor through Andhra Pradesh and Telangana has improved, but dozens of state highways and district roads remain charging deserts. If your route takes you off the major national highways — to a wedding in your hometown, a hillside resort, a village near your parents — you are on your own.

### Payment and UX Fragmentation
India has seven major public charging networks, each with its own app, RFID card, and pricing. A single charging stop can require downloading a new app if you are on an unfamiliar network. Cross-network payment roaming — where your one app works at any charger regardless of operator — is in pilot but not yet universal.

---

## What This Means for Your Next EV Journey

For city driving, India's charging network is genuinely sufficient for the vast majority of use cases. If you are driving within a major metro, home-charging overnight and topping up occasionally at a mall or office charger, the infrastructure is there.

For intercity travel on major corridors, it is workable with planning. The key word is planning. You need to know which chargers are on your route, which ones are fast enough to matter, and what your fallback is if one is down.

For everything else — rural trips, off-corridor routes, Tier 2 and 3 destinations — the infrastructure is still catching up. This is not a reason to avoid EVs. It is a reason to have software that knows the real state of the network, not just where chargers theoretically exist on a map.
